---
title: On failure after success
date: 2026-11-23
description: Everybody loves the underdog who finally wins. This is about the favorite who loses, and what it takes to begin again.
---

Everybody loves an underdog story. Rocky Balboa. Hanamichi Sakuragi from *Slam Dunk*. Brock Purdy, the last pick in the NFL Draft, making his name with the 49ers. And we love the lesson that comes with it: keep showing up, keep taking your losses, and the pain will mean something in the end. The struggle is beautiful because we already know how it ends.

We tell a different story much less often. What happens to the people who were supposed to win, and didn't?

## The favorite who loses

Take Gen.G at the 2026 *League of Legends* World Championship. They swept their quarterfinal and semifinal, then won the first game of the final against Bilibili Gaming. Then they lost the next three, and the title with them. For BLG, it was a story of triumph. For Gen.G, it was an ending they would have to live with.

That is the strange cruelty of competition: **one team's unforgettable victory is another team's memory they wish they could rewrite.**

We know what to tell someone who has always been losing: *don't give up, your time will come.* But what do you tell someone who has already been winning? "Work harder" sounds almost insulting when they already worked harder than ever. "Trust the process" sounds hollow when the process took them all the way to the final and still wasn't enough.

An underdog loses to being underestimated. A favorite loses their certainty. Then the questions start. Were you ever as good as people said? Was the progress real, or did the biggest match expose something you hadn't wanted to see?

## Let the loss be a loss

I think the temptation is to answer those questions with an excuse or a punishment. Call it bad luck and learn nothing, or decide that everything before it was worthless and tear yourself apart. Neither gets you very far.

Maybe rebuilding starts somewhere less dramatic: letting the loss be a loss. Not a blessing in disguise. Not a motivational speech waiting to happen. Just something you wanted badly and didn't get. You can be disappointed. You can be angry. You don't have to turn it into a lesson the moment it's over.

Then, eventually, you look at it clearly. Not to prove that you're a failure, but to understand **where you failed**. One turns a result into an identity. The other gives you something to work on. Usually the truth is less comforting, and more useful, than the first story you tell yourself.

## No music in the background

Understanding the loss doesn't bring the hunger back. I think that's the hardest part. The next practice doesn't come with a crowd. The next attempt doesn't come with a promise that this time will be different. There's no music swelling to tell you a comeback has started. There's just another day, another small thing to improve, another choice to show up.

Maybe that's how confidence returns: by collecting evidence, little by little, that you can still learn, adjust, and do the work. Confidence built on the belief that you *cannot* lose is fragile. Confidence built on knowing you can lose and continue is harder to shake.

## No promised ending

And we have to admit something: **there may never be a championship at the end of this.** Hard work improves your chances; it doesn't sign a contract with the future. Sometimes you give everything, come painfully close, and still don't get what you came for. So the harder question isn't whether you can win. It's whether you can still care enough to try when trying might hurt again.

Most of us will never stand on a Worlds stage, but we know smaller versions of that morning after. The exam you were sure you'd pass. The job you thought was yours. The project you poured months into, falling apart.

Maybe the answer isn't to become who you were before the loss. Maybe it's to become someone who no longer needs a guaranteed win to keep moving. That strength is quieter than the one we celebrate. It doesn't look like lifting a trophy. It looks like opening your notes again. Rewatching the match you don't want to watch. Going back to practice while the disappointment is still there.

The underdog who finally wins is a good story. The favorite who loses and chooses to begin again is just as human, and maybe just as brave.

Not because they know the next ending will be better.

**Because they don't—and they try anyway.**
